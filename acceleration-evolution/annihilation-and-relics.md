# Annihilation, pair production, and possible relics

This note examines whether particle annihilation helps explain a proposed collision of universe-scale bodies. It distinguishes established reactions from calculations the proposal still needs. See the [study README](README.md) for the wider acceleration work.

## The reverse reaction is known

Annihilation changes the particles carrying energy. It does not destroy energy. An electron and positron can produce two photons, and two photons can produce an electron and positron:

$$
e^-+e^+ \leftrightarrow \gamma+\gamma.
$$

Breit and Wheeler calculated the reverse reaction in 1934 [1]. Energy, momentum, electric charge, and angular momentum must balance. Particle number and total rest mass need not remain fixed. Antimatter has positive mass-energy, rather than negative energy that cancels ordinary matter.

For photon energies $E_1,E_2$ and collision angle $\theta$, define the squared centre-of-momentum energy as $s_E$. With the speed of light $c$ explicit,

$$
s_E=2E_1E_2(1-\cos\theta)\ge(2m_ec^2)^2.
$$

Thus head-on photons require $E_1E_2\ge m_e^2c^4$. Equal photon energies each need at least $m_ec^2\simeq511$ keV. These statements concern the kinematic threshold, not a guarantee of a large reaction rate.

An isolated real photon cannot create this massive pair in empty flat spacetime. Its four-momentum has zero invariant mass, while the pair has positive invariant mass. A second photon, a recoiling nucleus, or an external field changes the momentum budget. These are distinct experimental arrangements.

SLAC E144 observed positrons in 1997 [2]. Electrons first scattered laser photons to high energies. A high-energy photon then interacted with several laser photons to create a pair. This was evidence for nonlinear, multiphoton production.

STAR measured electron–positron pairs in encounters where fast gold ions passed without ordinary nuclear overlap [3]. The pair distributions matched photon fusion associated with the ions' electromagnetic fields. These photons are treated as quasi-real in the equivalent-photon description. This experiment should not be described as two independently prepared gamma-ray beams colliding in vacuum.

## Creation and annihilation in an expanding universe

A hot plasma can create and annihilate particles at the same time. In thermal equilibrium, the forward and reverse rates balance statistically. For a simple symmetric relic species $\chi$, a common number-density equation is

$$
\frac{dn_\chi}{dt}+3Hn_\chi
=-\langle\sigma v\rangle\left(n_\chi^2-n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}^2\right).
$$

Here $H$ measures expansion, $n_{\chi,\mathrm{eq}}$ is the equilibrium density, and $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ measures the thermally averaged reaction strength.

Both sides have units of number density per time. This convention suits a self-conjugate species. Distinct particle and antiparticle densities require coupled equations or adjusted factors, depending on whether $n_\chi$ counts one species or both.

As expansion and cooling reduce reaction rates, particles once in equilibrium can survive through thermal freeze-out. A particle with much weaker interactions may never reach equilibrium. Rare reactions or decays can instead build its abundance through freeze-in. Hall and colleagues developed this latter mechanism [4]. Neither mechanism establishes which particle, if any, constitutes dark matter.

Pair creation produces equal matter and antimatter. It cannot alone explain a net excess of baryons, the particles that include protons and neutrons. A symmetric starting model needs a process that generates this excess.

Under the usual assumptions, Sakharov's conditions require baryon-number violation, C and CP symmetry violation, and departure from thermal equilibrium [5]. An assumed initial asymmetry must also be stated and tracked.

## What the collision proposal must add

A collision can redistribute energy into many outgoing particles. That fact does not establish the creation of multiple expanding universes. The proposal needs definitions of its incoming bodies, what makes them “opposite,” and the geometry in which they interact. Separate spacetimes need a specified interaction before “collision” has a mathematical meaning.

Dark matter also needs a physical definition. Ordinary atoms or antimatter left after incomplete annihilation remain ordinary interacting material. A proposed dark species needs a mass, interactions, lifetime, abundance, and velocity distribution that allow the observed structures to form.

For reference, Planck's base $\Lambda$CDM fit gives $\Omega_ch^2\simeq0.120$ and $\Omega_bh^2\simeq0.0224$, about 5.4 times as much cold dark matter as baryonic matter [7]. This is a cosmological fit, not a particle identification. Planck also constrains energy deposited by dark-matter annihilation, with limits that depend on the particle model.

Matter and antimatter domains touching inside the observable universe would produce annihilation radiation. Calculations by Cohen, De Rújula and Glashow strongly constrain conventional symmetric-domain models through gamma rays and the cosmic microwave background [6].

Those calculations do not exclude every imagined causally disconnected antimatter universe.

## A calculation that could test part of the idea

A tractable next step is to specify two colliding radiation or plasma shells, ordinary gravity, and one stable dark particle. Evolve production, annihilation, expansion, and entropy. Compare the resulting dark-particle abundance with a clearly defined thermal history having matched initial energy and charges. This tests whether the collision changes the relic yield.

Before fitting parameters, set observable failure criteria: excess annihilation radiation, incompatible light-element abundances, excessive particle velocities, or a wrong dark-to-baryon ratio.

A separate gravitational calculation must demonstrate whether multiple expanding regions form rather than a hot plasma or gravitational collapse. A distinctive predicted signal, with a stated exclusion threshold, would make the broader proposal testable.
